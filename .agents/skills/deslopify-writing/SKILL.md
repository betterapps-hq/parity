# Deslopify writing

Make the text specific, useful and natural for its audience. Preserve the intended facts, qualifications and
commitments while revising empty or repetitive expression. Use the requested target voice; a brand rewrite can
change how the draft sounds. Review requests receive suggestions, not file changes. Edit files only when requested.

## Exact patterns and phrases

Check the specific words and phrases below, not just their category names. The pattern library is maintained here
so writing and voice skills can use the same checks.

### Patterns found in anti-slop writing guides

- Rhetorical questions such as “What does this mean for you?” or “The real question is …” when a direct statement is available.
- Fake-depth phrases such as “at its core,” “what really matters,” “the deeper issue,” or “the real challenge.”
- Manufactured subtlety: “quietly” used as dramatic filler, especially in “quietly transformed,” “quietly reshaped,” “quietly redefined,” or “quietly became.” It implies unnoticed progress or hidden significance without establishing it. Related constructions include “silently revolutionized,” “subtly reshaped,” and “unassumingly became.” Remove the adverb when it adds no factual meaning; explain the actual change. Keep “quietly” when it describes relevant low volume, discretion, or an established lack of attention. See the [examples and exceptions](references/examples.md#manufactured-subtlety-with-quietly).
- Dramatic colon setups such as “One thing is clear: …” or “Here’s what nobody tells you: …”
- Promotional puffery such as “renowned,” “vibrant,” “transformative,” “powerful,” “game-changing,” “rich,” or “evolving” when the fact can be stated plainly.
- Inflated metaphors such as “landscape,” “journey,” “unlock,” “navigate,” “tapestry,” or “roadmap” for ordinary work.
- “It is crucial to note,” “it is important to remember,” or “it bears mentioning.” State the fact or instruction.
- Mechanical rule-of-three strings, especially three adjectives or three abstract nouns in a row.
- The same sentence length and structure repeated across a paragraph. Vary the rhythm naturally.
- Bold lead-ins on every bullet, followed by a colon and a full explanation. Use plain bullets or prose.
- A polished general statement that never names a person, product, date, amount, or next action.
- A sudden shift from the author's established voice into formal, academic, or promotional language without a reason grounded in the audience or purpose.
- A conclusion that turns an ordinary event or practical point into a broad lesson about trust, partnership, or the future without support from the text.
- “Elevate” used as generic praise, as in “elevate your workflow” or “elevate the experience,” without naming what improves. State the supported change; keep literal or precise technical meanings.
- “In conclusion” used as a canned ending, especially in short replies where the ending is already obvious. Give the conclusion directly. Keep an explicit closing signal when the format or a long spoken argument makes it useful.

### Announcing significance

Watch for “This speaks volumes,” “This underscores the importance of,” “serves as a powerful reminder,” and
“marks a significant step.” These phrases announce that something matters before explaining why. State the
supported consequence or inference directly. Keep a substantive interpretation when it helps the reader, but don't
substitute a declaration of importance for evidence.

### Conversational performance

Watch for “Let’s unpack that,” “Let that sink in,” and “And honestly?” These phrases can perform conversation,
depth, or candor without adding any. Begin the explanation, let the fact carry its own weight, or state the opinion.
Keep a genuine invitation to examine something together, or a deliberate spoken beat, when it serves the context.

### Tacked-on significance

Watch for endings such as “…paving the way for,” “…ushering in a new era,” “…shaping the future of,” and
“…a testament to its…”. They often inflate an otherwise complete statement with an unsupported future or symbolic
meaning. End at the last useful fact, or explain the supported consequence and its conditions. Keep actual enabling
relationships and historically justified descriptions; don't add predictions merely to give a sentence a grand finish.

One phrase is not proof that text was generated. Look for a cluster of repeated patterns, generic wording, missing
specifics and a voice that does not fit the intended writer or brand. A requested transformation is an intentional
register change. Do not assign an AI-detection score or claim to identify who or what wrote the text.

Do not force these rules when a phrase sounds natural in context. The goal is clear, specific writing suited to the
author, audience and purpose; length and register depend on the work.

## Visual copy rules

- Don't use dots as visual separators, including `·`, `•`, or periods used decoratively between labels. Use spacing, separate text elements, or a clearly worded phrase instead. For example, replace `Fast · Flexible · Open source` with separate labels without separator glyphs. This rule does not prohibit sentence punctuation, decimal points, or bullets in actual lists.
- Don't number eyebrow labels above headings. Use the label itself: `Features`, not `01 / Features` or `01. Features`. This rule concerns the short label above a heading, not numbered instructions or meaningful numbers in the content.

## Review and revise

1. Read for meaning first. Identify the point, factual claims, conditions and intended reader action. Keep what works.
2. Read the [broader pattern catalog](references/patterns.md). Assess recurring habits across sentences and paragraphs,
   not just isolated words. Use the [example judgments](references/examples.md) when deciding whether a construction
   adds meaning or merely repeats a template.
   For marketing copy, also apply the catalog's [marketing copy editing checks](references/patterns.md#marketing-copy-editing-checks), including the punctuation and repetition limits and their exceptions.
3. For each useful finding, show the exact excerpt, explain the actual reader-facing problem and propose the smallest
   effective correction. Group repeated instances. If specificity needs a missing fact, identify it rather than inventing one.
4. Rework the expression from its underlying facts. Replacing "powerful" with another praising adjective supplies no
   missing capability. Preserve accurate technical meanings, useful questions, actual sets of three and deliberate rhetoric.
5. Read the result as a whole. Keep the chosen voice and useful cadence. Avoid replacing every sentence with a short
   fragment, inventing anecdotes, adding deliberate mistakes or forcing slang to make the result seem human.

For a point edit, stay within the requested passage and its necessary context. For a requested rewrite, return clean
copy in the requested format, with only the explanation the task needs. Compare factual scope, certainty and promises
with the original before delivery. Quoted material stays verbatim unless its modification is requested.

## Apply alongside a writing guide

Pattern findings describe problems with expression, not a substitute editorial standard. Parallel headings,
consistent technical terms, defined term lists and input-neutral instructions may intentionally repeat structures.
A metaphor can clarify a subject; a question can invite a real decision; humor can carry an insight. Apply the other
guide's placement-specific conventions and judge what the device accomplishes here.

## Grow the library through examples

When asked to extend this library, record the observation in the relevant pattern category and add an example
showing the passage, its context,
the reader-facing problem and a revision grounded in the same facts. Include a useful counterexample or exception
when the construction can work. Start with an observed case; generalize only when a recurring failure supports it.
Extend an existing category when it already explains the problem, and keep literal words in examples instead of
replacing them with broad labels. One awkward phrase does not establish a universal ban.
