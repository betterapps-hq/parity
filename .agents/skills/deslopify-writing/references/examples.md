# Example judgments

These are illustrative editorial cases. Use the stated context to judge the construction; do not transfer invented
facts into real copy. Each example distinguishes an empty habit from useful information or a deliberate technique.

## Empty praise hides the available fact

Context: the only supplied capability is CSV imports for files under 100 MB.

Before: "At its core, our powerful platform unlocks a transformative journey. It is crucial to note that imports accept
CSV files under 100 MB."

After: "Import CSV files under 100 MB."

The opening supplies no capability, mechanism or evidence. The revision retains the file type and size condition;
it does not invent a faster import, easier setup or larger capacity.

Counterexample: a technical description may use "powerful" if the term has a defined meaning and the relevant
property is explained. The issue here is unsupported praise, not the letters in a word.

## Ceremony after a routine fact

Before: "The archive opened on Tuesday, marking a pivotal step in our shared journey."

After: "The archive opened on Tuesday."

The trailing clause increases emotional scale without explaining a consequence. If opening the archive has a real
effect worth explaining, state that supported effect instead of inventing one.

Counterexample: a conclusion can explain a significant consequence when the preceding evidence supports it.

## Manufactured subtlety with “quietly”

“Quietly” can supply narrative significance instead of information. It makes an ordinary action sound unnoticed,
understated, or unexpectedly consequential without establishing those qualities. Inspect both the adverb and the
verb: removing “quietly” does not make an unsupported claim of transformation specific or accurate.

| Before | Problem | Revision or next step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “While I was busy working, AI quietly transformed our docs.” | Implies an unnoticed, significant change without explaining what changed. | “While I was working, AI updated our docs.” Name the specific updates when known; retain a claim of substantial transformation only when supported. |
| “This feature quietly became essential.” | Implies an unnoticed rise in importance without evidence of use or dependence. | “We now use this feature in every release,” if supported. Otherwise, establish how it is used before making a stronger claim. |
| “The update quietly fixes a major frustration.” | Suggests the fix received little attention, although that may be irrelevant; the problem remains unnamed. | Name the actual problem the update fixes. For example, if confirmed: “The update fixes the crash when opening large files.” |
| “These small decisions quietly shape the entire experience.” | Implies hidden influence without naming a mechanism. | Explain which decisions affect which part of the experience. Don't invent the decisions or their effects. |

The example revisions are conditional illustrations, not facts to insert into unrelated copy. Don't replace
“quietly” with “silently” or “subtly” and leave the same unsupported implication intact.

Keep the word when it carries relevant, supported meaning:

- “She spoke quietly so she wouldn't wake the baby.” It describes volume and explains why it matters.
- “They quietly arranged a farewell gift to keep it a surprise.” It describes intentional discretion, provided that
  intention is established in the account.
- “The company quietly changed the policy” can convey an established lack of announcement or attention when that
  absence matters. Prefer the precise fact when available: “The company changed the policy without notifying
  customers.” Use that revision only if the lack of notification is verified; absence of publicity alone doesn't prove it.

Ask what “quietly” adds that the reader needs to know. Keep a meaningful description; remove manufactured drama.

## A negation communicates a real default

Passage: "It's not enabled by default; turn it on in Settings."

Judgment: keep the negation in an anti-slop review. It names a state and tells the reader how to change it. It is not
a manufactured reversal. An editorial guide may separately prescribe different terminology for the same meaning.

Counterexample: "It's not a tool. It's a revolution." introduces a grander label without explaining a distinction.
Replace it with the actual capability or consequence when known.

## Three items are the actual structure

Passage: "Core packages define behavior, React bindings connect it to React, and component adapters supply the UI."

Judgment: the three items describe three distinct architectural layers. Keep all three when relevant. Mechanical
rule-of-three checks concern decorative padding, such as three interchangeable praising adjectives.

Counterexample: when only two distinct benefits are supported, adding a third vague aspiration weakens the copy.

## A contrast gives the reader an insight

Context: people at separate desks edit the same shared document.

Passage: "Different desks. One draft."

Judgment: the contrast expresses a real relationship. Keep the idea when it serves the placement, and apply the
chosen style guide's heading or sentence punctuation separately. An anti-slop pass should not erase the contrast
simply because it uses two short clauses.

## Repetition helps readers find comparable information

Context: a help page has procedure headings for creating a project, adding a member and resetting a preference.

Judgment: parallel action headings help scanning. They differ from a page of paragraphs that all repeat the same
setup, reveal and exaggerated conclusion. Apply an editorial guide's deliberate heading and term-list formats.

Counterexample: bold labels followed by long explanations on every bullet can add visual clutter when the bullets
are ordinary prose rather than definitions or navigational aids.

## A familiar word is literal or technically precise

Passages: "The map shows the coastal landscape." "Use the navigation menu to move between sections."

Judgment: inspect their actual meaning. A literal landscape is not an inflated metaphor. A technical use of
navigation can be precise; a chosen terminology guide may still prefer a more specific or simpler term.

Counterexample: "Navigate the evolving landscape of success" names neither a concrete setting nor an action.

## Generic improvement with “elevate”

Context: a tool assigns each imported CSV row to a reviewer.

Before: “Elevate your review workflow by assigning each imported row to a reviewer.”

After: “Assign each imported row to a reviewer.”

The capability already communicates the useful action; “elevate” adds no defined improvement. Keep precise uses,
such as “Elevate the process privileges,” when privilege elevation is the intended technical operation.

## A canned ending with “In conclusion”

Context: a short reply explains that the reader must save changes before closing a window.

Before: “In conclusion, save your changes before closing the window.”

After: “Save your changes before closing the window.”

The transition adds ceremony to a short instruction. In a long speech, an explicit closing signal can orient the
audience; don't remove useful structure merely because the phrase is familiar.

## Announcing significance

Before: “The missed deadline serves as a powerful reminder of the importance of testing.”

Judgment: the sentence asserts a lesson without explaining the connection between testing and the delay. If the
account establishes that testing found a release-blocking defect, name that connection. Otherwise, don't invent it.
Apply the same check to “This speaks volumes,” “This underscores the importance of,” and “marks a significant step.”

Counterexample: interpreting evidence is useful in analysis. Keep the supported inference, such as “The test found
a defect that blocks release,” when established; the problem is the stock announcement replacing that inference.

## Conversational performance

Before: “The import accepts CSV files under 100 MB. Let that sink in.”

After: “The import accepts CSV files under 100 MB.”

The command to pause gives an ordinary limit artificial weight. “Let’s unpack that” can similarly delay an
explanation, and “And honestly?” can advertise candor without changing the substance.

Counterexample: in a workshop, “Let’s unpack that assumption together” can be a real invitation followed by group
discussion. A spoken pause can also serve an actual rhetorical purpose. Judge the action and context.

## Tacked-on significance

Before: “The update adds CSV export, paving the way for a new era of collaboration.”

After: “The update adds CSV export.”

The ending invents a broad consequence from one capability. Apply the same check to “ushering in a new era,”
“shaping the future of,” and “a testament to its.” Don't replace one inflated ending with another.

Counterexample: if CSV export enables a documented import into a particular tool, explaining that relationship
helps readers. State the supported compatibility and any conditions directly. Historical writing may also justify
an era-level claim with evidence; a routine feature announcement usually supplies no such basis.

## Preserve qualifications while tightening

Before: "The result holds for positive integers if the sequence is finite."

Bad edit: "The result holds for integers."

Judgment: keep both conditions. The shorter sentence changes the claim. Concision does not justify removing facts,
scope or uncertainty that the reader needs.

## Changing a voice is deliberate

Context: the user asks to change a hesitant draft into confident product copy. The stated capability is assigning
each imported CSV row to a reviewer.

Before: "Well, I guess you can ask someone to look at a row, if that helps."

After: "Assign each imported row to a reviewer."

The register change is requested and preserves the capability. It should not be flagged merely because the result
sounds different from the draft. For ordinary cleanup without a requested voice change, retain effective personal style.
