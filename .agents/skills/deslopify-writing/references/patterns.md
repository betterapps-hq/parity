# Patterns to examine

Use these as diagnostic questions, not a blacklist. Establish a pattern's actual effect on this passage before
suggesting a change. A single conventional phrase may be natural; a whole page built from the same devices can be tiring.
The examples below are illustrative, not claims to insert into a real draft.

For the exact words and phrases to check, read [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md). Apply those checks alongside this broader catalog.

## Meaning and emphasis

| Pattern | What to examine | Useful correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stage-setting opener | Does the opening announce importance before supplying information? | Start with the event, decision or answer the reader came for. |
| Manufactured reversal | Does a negation introduce an alternative nobody raised? | State the useful claim directly. Keep contrasts that clarify a real misunderstanding. |
| Question answered by its author | Does the question organize an actual reader concern, or just manufacture suspense? | Give the answer directly when the question adds no value. Keep genuine FAQs. |
| Importance without consequence | Is a routine fact described as historic or transformative without evidence? | Explain the supported consequence at its actual scale. |
| Empty conclusion | Does the ending add a decision or action, or repeat the thesis at greater emotional volume? | Finish at the last useful point. Retain a recap when readers need one to act. |
| Invented conceptual label | Does an impressive name add a useful distinction or merely rename an ordinary problem? | Describe the mechanism; keep established domain vocabulary. |
| Hollow range | Are the endpoints comparable and informative? | Name the actual cases or scope instead of implying breadth. |
| False balance | Are two positions made to sound equally supported just for symmetry? | Preserve the actual evidence and uncertainty. |

## Evidence and specificity

| Pattern | What to examine | Useful correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Unnamed authority | Who supports the claim, and is the attribution available? | Name an existing source or flag the missing evidence. Don't invent an expert. |
| Floating statistic | Does a number have a source, denominator and relevant period? | Preserve verified context; request missing context before presenting it as evidence. |
| Empty modifier | Does a word describe a measurable feature or just praise it? | State the feature when known; delete unsupported praise. |
| Inflated certainty | Does the claim actually support words such as every, guaranteed or never? | Restore the supported scope without weakening genuine requirements. |
| Abstract actor | Can the reader tell who performs the action? | Name the actor if known. Keep passive voice when the actor is unknown or irrelevant. |
| Decorative explanation | Does a trailing clause explain a causal link or merely announce significance? | State the link explicitly when supported; otherwise cut the empty commentary. |
| Ambiguous reference | Does this, it or they have more than one possible antecedent? | Name the object once where ambiguity arises. |

## Rhythm and structure

| Pattern | What to examine | Useful correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Repeated opening | Do nearby sentences all begin with the same phrase without a rhetorical purpose? | Combine related points or vary structure around their meaning. |
| Forced enumeration | Are items present because they are distinct, or because the sentence wants three beats? | Keep the actual set; don't add or remove substance to satisfy a rhythm. |
| Uniform sentence shape | Does each paragraph use the same setup, elaboration and punchline? | Let the information determine sentence and paragraph length. |
| Fragmented drama | Do isolated words or tiny sentences create emphasis without information? | Reconnect the thought when fragmentation makes it harder to follow. |
| Nested digressions | Do parentheses, dashes and qualifiers interrupt the main point repeatedly? | Move essential qualifications beside the claim; remove only irrelevant detours. |
| Mechanical transitions | Does each paragraph open by announcing an addition or a conclusion? | Show the relationship through the substance when it is already clear. |
| Repeated summary | Is the same point previewed, explained and restated without a reader need? | Keep the form that best serves this reader. |

## Voice and presentation

- Check for an unexplained register shift within a passage. Formal, academic, promotional or intimate language can
  be appropriate when it fits the genre, audience and author's established voice.
- Look for generic empathy, false vulnerability and invented personal experience. Keep sincere acknowledgments that
  fit the situation, but never manufacture a story or emotion for the writer.
- Consider whether an analogy explains an unfamiliar idea or patronizes a knowledgeable reader. Preserve useful ones.
- Examine dense bolding, heading-heavy short replies and bullets that all start with labels. Keep formatting when it
  helps navigation; use paragraphs when the ideas depend on one another.
- Remove leftover drafting instructions, assistant introductions and offers to continue from a finished deliverable.
- Watch repeated stock metaphors and fashionable verbs, but keep words used in their precise literal or technical sense.
  A geographic landscape or an ecosystem in an ecology report needs no replacement.

## What to build toward

Give each paragraph a clear job. Put related conditions and actions together. Choose verbs that reveal what happens.
Use concrete nouns where the facts permit them. Retain enough context for a first-time reader while avoiding a repeat
of what this audience already knows. Prefer ordinary words when they express the same meaning; don't replace precise
technical language with vague simplicity.

Let purpose determine the structure: instructions need usable steps, an argument needs evidence and reasoning, and
a narrative may need pacing or suspense. Don't force every piece into a direct-answer template or add a call to action
when none is needed.

## Example judgments

- "The archive opened on Tuesday, marking a pivotal step in our shared journey."
  The second clause adds unsupported ceremony. Suggest "The archive opened on Tuesday."
- "It's not enabled by default; turn it on in Settings."
  Keep the negation: it states a real default and the action needed to change it.
- "The result holds for positive integers if the sequence is finite."
  Keep both conditions. Shortening this to "The result holds for integers" changes the claim.
- "The manuscript was damaged in the flood."
  Passive voice is reasonable when the responsible actor is unknown. Don't invent one to make the sentence active.
- "The draft feels bloated."
  In a review, identify the passage and what can be removed. A taste judgment alone doesn't help the author edit.

## Additional recurring constructions

| Signal | What to assess | Useful correction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "It's not X, it's Y" or "Not because X. Because Y." | Does it clarify a real distinction or invent an alternative for drama? | State the supported point directly when the reversal adds nothing. |
| "No X, no Y, no Z" | Do the absences help a decision or substitute rhythm for an explanation? | Explain what actually happens. Keep material terms such as "No credit card required" when true. |
| "The result?" or "The best part:" | Does the setup delay a straightforward fact? | Give the result directly when the question or reveal adds nothing. |
| "In today's fast-paced world," "Whether you're X or Y," "Here's the thing," "Say goodbye to," "X, reimagined," "Unlock the power of" | Could the opening introduce almost any subject unchanged? | Open with this subject's event, problem, mechanism or supported possibility. Keep audience distinctions when they explain a real difference. |
| "Seamless," "robust," "streamline," "empower" | Does the word name an actual property or hide missing information? | State the relevant setup, failure behavior, removed step or capability when known. Keep precise domain meanings. |
| A claim followed by "..., ensuring ..." or a pile of comma clauses | Does the tail add information or overstate and repeat the claim? | Keep new facts and their conditions. Remove empty commentary or separate a distinct thought. |

## Marketing copy editing checks

Apply these additional editing conventions to marketing copy. The punctuation and frequency limits are house-style
constraints for this placement, not evidence of AI authorship or universal rules for prose.

- Avoid em dashes in headlines, subheads, calls to action, ads, social posts, and subject lines. Use a period, comma,
  or colon where appropriate. Limit em dashes in long marketing copy to one or two per page.
- Limit decorative sentence fragments and rhetorical lists of three to one of each per section, such as a hero,
  page section, or short post. CTA microcopy and actual sets of three features are exempt. Don't remove factual items
  to meet the limit.
- Treat “seamless,” “robust,” “powerful,” “streamline,” and “empower” as placeholders when they supply vague praise.
  Replace them with the supported capability or mechanism. If two such placeholders appear in one paragraph,
  rewrite the paragraph from its facts. Preserve precise technical meanings.
- Apply the recurring-construction checks above to every edit. For “It's not X, it's Y” and “Not because X. Because Y,”
  state the useful point and reason directly when the contrast is manufactured. For “No setup call, no templates,
  no waiting on IT,” explain what happens; retain a material absence such as “No card required” when true.
- Remove self-answered setups such as “The result? 3x faster” and colon reveals such as “The best part: it learns”
  when they merely delay the statement. Retain any numerical claim only if supported. Genuine FAQs and questions
  the reader actually needs answered are exempt.
- Cut trailing restatements such as “…the data you already have, no exports, no spreadsheets” or “…ensuring nothing
  slips.” End at the claim or give a distinct benefit its own sentence. Keep clauses that add a supported new fact,
  such as “Imports run nightly, keeping your existing IDs.”

Rebuild weak copy from the mechanism, customer, action, or supported result rather than swapping synonyms. When a
needed fact is missing, identify the gap; an editorial draft can use `[NEED: ...]` rather than inventing evidence.
Keep the requested register: a formal sentence that makes a specific claim does not need artificial informality.
