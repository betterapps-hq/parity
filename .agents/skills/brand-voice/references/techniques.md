# Voice techniques

Choose a technique because it fits the message and intended effect. These devices are options, not requirements to
repeat across every paragraph. Each worked example uses an invented brief and only the facts stated with it. Use the
examples to understand the move, not as claims or templates to transplant into real copy.

## Reveal

### Make the understatement of the century

Let a small, concrete observation carry the weight of a larger subject. The contrast between modest wording and the
facts creates the effect. Don't minimize harm or dismiss the reader's concern.

Worked example: volunteers have made 40,000 handwritten letters searchable by name.
"A little catching up on the mail. Forty thousand handwritten letters, now searchable by name."
The modest opening lets the scale of the work supply the surprise; the next sentence explains it.

### Leave the question unanswered

Leave room for the reader to consider a genuine uncertainty or possibility. An open thought can invite participation.
Don't withhold a fact needed to act, end every section with a question or fabricate suspense where a direct answer fits.

Worked example: a library offers a recording room to local storytellers.
"The room is ready. The next story is yours. Record it at the library."
The invitation leaves the story open while still naming the available service.

### Draw back the curtain

Show a less obvious part of the subject: an overlooked participant, hidden constraint or consequence. Make the insight
specific enough to change understanding. Don't announce a profound revelation and then restate the obvious.

Worked example: a city tree map helps people plan walks along shaded streets.
"The trees are part of your route, too. Use the map to plan a walk in the shade."
An overlooked part of a route becomes the reason to use the map.

### Dream out loud

List concrete capabilities, actions or possibilities in an imagined future. The accumulating details let the reader
picture what could happen. Ground the hypothetical in real capabilities, and distinguish possibility from a promise.
Concrete actions carry more meaning than a list of aspirational adjectives.

Worked example: a community workshop offers workbenches, woodworking tools and guidance on Saturdays.
"A shelf for the kitchen. A bench for the hall. Saturday could start at our workbench. Tools and guidance are here."
The possible projects accumulate into a picture of the day without promising a finished object or a completion time.

## Inspire

### Make opposites attractive

Use a meaningful tension between contrasting ideas to make a point memorable. The relationship must hold up on
inspection. A useful contrast names something real; an automatic "not this, but that" construction adds little.

Worked example: remote collaborators can edit the same document together.
"Different desks. One draft. Write together in a shared document."
Physical separation and a shared result give the contrast substance.

### Make our vernacular spectacular

Use a term from the brand's actual domain in a fresh but intelligible setting. It should connect the brand's work to
the message, rather than adding jargon as decoration. Don't let wordplay misrepresent a technical meaning.

Worked example: a software education project licenses its lessons for adaptation and redistribution.
"Good lessons welcome a fork. Adapt ours and share your version."
A familiar software action becomes an invitation to participate in education; the license makes the invitation true.

### Marvel at a simple truth

Find genuine wonder in a supported fact or human outcome. Let the audience recognize what is remarkable instead of
asking it to admire the organization. Avoid an inflated adjective when the fact itself carries the interest.

Worked example: two people started a project that now has 60 volunteer maintainers.
"Sixty people now caring for something two people started. Meet the maintainers."
The human fact carries the wonder and directs attention to the contributors.

## Provoke

### Make vulnerability a strength

Acknowledge a real worry, limitation or difficult emotion and connect it to a credible response or action. Don't
invent the author's vulnerability, claim to know how everyone feels or use another person's hardship as a flourish.

Worked example: beginner computer classes explicitly welcome repeated questions and let learners retry tasks.
"You can ask the same question twice. Our beginner classes leave room to try again."
It answers a plausible fear of getting things wrong with a concrete practice, without claiming everyone feels afraid.

### Skewer the sacred

Challenge a genuinely held assumption with a contrary point of view. Use reason, emotion or humor to make the
alternative compelling. Judge the complete expression: the joke or emotional turn can carry the insight itself.
Keep its factual implications honest and avoid inventing an opponent or belief just to defeat it.

Worked example: a repairable desk lamp has a replaceable LED module.
"When the light goes out, keep the lamp. Replace the LED module."
The line challenges the habit of replacing the whole product by naming the alternative this product actually offers.

### Make it an exception that rules

Present a different way of approaching a familiar situation and explain its appeal. A contrast can distinguish the
brand's choice without caricaturing people who choose otherwise or inventing superiority.

Worked example: a lamp shop sells replacement modules and helps owners repair their lamps.
"The best lamp might be the one you already own. We sell replacement modules and help you repair it."
Keeping an existing lamp becomes preferable to buying new, without unsupported claims about competing shops.

### Make it all or nothing

Use an unequivocal statement when expressing a sincerely held value. Distinguish a value or ambition from a factual
guarantee. Don't turn a limited capability or uncertain outcome into an absolute promise.

Worked example: the brand commits to selling replacement parts for every lamp it sells for ten years after purchase.
"Every lamp. Parts for ten years. We commit to selling replacement parts for ten years after you buy your lamp."
The absolute applies to the stated commitment, not to a lifetime warranty or a guarantee the lamp cannot fail.

### Make it unflinching

State a supported cause, consequence or necessary action plainly, especially when evasion would weaken the point.
Be candid without assigning unsupported blame or flattening uncertainty into certainty.

Worked example: an event has moved from a venue with entrance steps to a venue with a step-free entrance.
"An invitation isn't enough if you can't get through the door. Our new venue has a step-free entrance."
The physical barrier explains the action. The copy does not claim that the new venue meets every accessibility need.

## Choosing among them

Start with the reader's need. Explanation may benefit from a hidden detail; an invitation may benefit from a concrete
possibility; an argument may benefit from a challenged assumption. Sometimes a plain sentence is the strongest choice.
Use as few devices as the idea needs, and assess the whole piece for repetitive rhythm or overstated emotion.
Give a creative line enough supporting copy to reveal its meaning. If the line could advertise unrelated products
unchanged, connect it to a fact or action that belongs to this subject. A factual body doesn't rescue an opening that
misleads the reader, but it can give an intriguing opening the detail needed to work.
