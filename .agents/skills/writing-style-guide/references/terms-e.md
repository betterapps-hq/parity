<a id="e-words"></a>

## e-words

```text
e-words


Don't create new words with e- (for electronic).

It's OK to use e-words, such as e-book and e-commerce, that are included in this guide
and in Merriam-Webster Dictionary            .

Almost all e-words are hyphenated. Email is an exception.

Lowercase the e in body text, and capitalize the E at the beginning of a sentence or a
heading. The letter following the hyphen is capitalized only when title-style
capitalization is required, which is rare in content in this style.

Examples
(Sentence-style capitalization) E-commerce and security
(Title-style capitalization) Strategic E-Commerce Systems and Tools for Competing in the
Digital Marketplace

See also Capitalization
```

## earlier

```text

If possible, use a hyperlink to help users find information elsewhere in the content. If

a piece of content.

Use or earlier or previous instead of or lower to refer to all versions of a product that
```

<a id="email"></a>

## email

```text
email


Use to refer generically to an electronic mail program, to refer collectively to email
messages, or to refer to individual email messages. If necessary to distinguish these
meanings, use email message or message to refer to an individual piece of email. Make
sure it's clear that you're not referring to instant messaging. After you have established
the context of electronic mail, it's OK to use mail instead of email.

It's OK to use email as a verb.

Global tip In some languages, the translation of email is different for noun and verb
forms. If you use email as a noun, provide enough context that localizers can identify its
role in the sentence. For example, write send an email, which positions email after a verb
and an article, where a noun is expected. In contrast, Email used by itself as a heading
could be a noun or a verb.

Use Email at the beginning of a sentence or heading. Never hyphenate email or
capitalize the m.

Examples
You have two new emails.
Scroll through email to find the message you want to read.
You have new mail.
Email us your comments.

See also message
```

<a id="emoji-emoticons"></a>

## emoji, emoticons

```text
emoji, emoticons


An emoji (plural emoji) is a small image that represents an emotion, concept, or object.
Examples include 😀 and ☹ .

An emoticon is a combination of keyboard characters that expresses an emotion or attitude.
Examples include :) for a smiling face and :( for a frowning face.

In social media posts and informal communications, emoji and emoticons add personality and
can help communicate meaning and tone.

Use emoji instead of emoticons. Emoji are better for accessibility because:

      Every emoji has a type of alt text that screen readers can read out to users.
      Screen readers don't interpret the combination of characters in an emoticon. Instead, they
      try to process the characters literally, ignore them, or indicate their presence only through
      a pause.

However, not all screen readers support emoji. Always ensure that your intended meaning and
tone get across without them.

General guidelines for emoji use

Follow these guidelines to use emoji in a way that's effective, positive, and accessible:

      Don't use emoji in content that's related to a serious topic. They might make the topic
      seem unserious or trivial.

      Don't use emoji in place of words.

      Don't use emoji in the middle of a sentence. Put them at the end of the sentence. Even
      better, put them at the end of the paragraph, message, or post.

      To keep emoji from losing their impact, don't overuse them.

      A general rule is to use a maximum of three emoji per message or post. Use a maximum
      of three emoji in a row, and always put a space between them.

      Don't use emoji at the beginning of consecutive lines to simulate bullets in a list.

      Don't embed emoji (or emoticons) in images.

Don't use emoji in profile names.

Guidelines for choosing emoji

      Limit yourself to popular emoji that are widely recognized and understood. Avoid
      ambiguous and obscure emoji.

      Ensure that the literal and figurative meanings of an emoji reflect your intended
      meaning. The literal meaning of every emoji is provided by a string that's called the
      Common Locale Data Repository (CLDR) short name or Unicode name. Screen readers use
      this name as alt text.

      In addition to its literal meaning, an emoji might have one or more figurative meanings.
      Make sure that both types of meaning are consistent with what you're trying to convey.

      Tip You can find the CLDR short name for every emoji on sites such as Unicode.org                       and
      Emojipedia.org       .

      Consider potential cultural and diversity implications, and avoid any emoji that might
      be inappropriate. The connotations of an emoji can vary across cultures and
      communities. What one group considers innocent, friendly, or humorous might be
      offensive to another group. To learn more, see Bias-free communication and Global
      communications.

      Note Emoji (and emoticons) might be removed from localized versions of your content.

      When you feel you can, use the standard version of emoji. Custom emoji that represent
      different skin tones are great for inclusiveness, but they can have longer alt text and take
      longer for screen readers to read.

      Use emoji that work across devices. The appearance of an emoji can vary across
      platforms and devices. Websites such as Emojipedia.org                 show the variations of each
      emoji. Avoid emoji that might appear to users in a form that changes or undermines your
      intended meaning.


 Last updated on 04/10/2026
```

<a id="enable-enables-enabled"></a>

## enable, enables, enabled

```text
enable, enables, enabled


Avoid using enables (or allows or lets) to refer to things that technology makes possible for the
customer. Rewrite to focus on what's important or possible from the customer's point of view. For
example, use "Write, draw, and take notes on webpages by using Microsoft Edge" instead of
"Microsoft Edge enables you to write, draw, and take notes on webpages."

Don't use enable to describe making a feature, setting, command, or function available, unless
you need to match the UI. Use turn on.

Don't use enabled to describe the state of a feature, setting, command, or function, unless you
need to match the UI. Use on, turned on, or available.

In UI, avoid using Enable/Disable as labels for a toggle that controls availability. Use On/Off, if
those words are accurate for the feature or capability. Or use more specific words, such as
Show/Hide, Add/Remove, or Allow/Block.

Example
After your administrator turns on the feature, verify that the button is available.

See also
disable, disables, disabled (functionality)
turn on, turn off


 Last updated on 04/28/2026
```

## ensure, insure, assure

```text
ensure, insure, assure


Ensure, insure, and assure are interchangeable in many situations. To improve worldwide
readability, this style makes these distinctions:

      Use ensure to mean to make sure or to guarantee.
      Use insure to mean to provide insurance.
      Use assure to mean to state positively or to make confident.
```

<a id="euro"></a>

## euro

```text
euro


Lowercase.

Plural: euros

Symbol: €

In US content, place the euro symbol in front of the amount. Different locales might
place the euro symbol elsewhere, as appropriate. A euro is divided into 100 cents. When
expressing an amount in euros and cents in US content, use a decimal point. Different
locales might use a decimal point or a comma, as appropriate.

Example
€3.50

Use supports the euro currency standard to refer to a country's use of the euro.

Use the following phrases to refer to countries that have adopted the euro as their
currency:

      European Union (EU) members trading in euros
      European Union (EU) members that have adopted the euro
      euro nations
      members of the Economic and Monetary Union (EMU)

Use references to the EMU cautiously. Some people aren't familiar with the organization.

Note On subsequent mentions, it's OK to use the abbreviations EU and EMU. It's also OK
to refer to EU members as EU member states and to EMU members as EMU member
states.

Use non-euro nations to refer to EU member states that haven't adopted the euro as
their currency.

The terms euroland and eurozone are OK to use on websites with an informal tone.
Don't use these terms in product documentation or other formal contexts, especially if
the content will be localized.

See also Currency
```

<a id="executable-exe"></a>

## executable, .exe

```text
executable, .exe


Use executable and .exe as adjectives, not nouns. Use an, not a, as the article preceding
.exe: an .exe file.

Examples
an executable program
the .exe file

Use executable file only in content for a technical audience. For a general audience, use
program file instead.
```
